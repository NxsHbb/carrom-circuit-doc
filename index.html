Carrom Circuit

A complete offline carrom board game for Android and iOS, built with Flutter and Flame.

Introduction

Carrom Circuit is a full carrom game you can rebrand and publish as your own. It runs entirely offline — there is no backend, no login and no server cost. Everything a player earns is stored on their device.

What is included

  • Realistic, deterministic carrom physics written from scratch in pure Dart
  • Tournament — white vs black, queen and cover, full foul handling
  • Scramble — a points race where any coin scores
  • Pass & Play for two players on a single device
  • Solo Match against the robot, with Easy, Medium and Hard opponents
  • Prize Tables — a 12-table ladder played for virtual coins, with a matchmaking screen
  • Virtual coin economy: daily rewards, rewarded-video coins, win and loss settlement
  • Cosmetic equipment shop: strikers, boards and coin sets
  • Google AdMob interstitial and rewarded video, frequency capped
  • Sound effects, haptics, settings and a polished menu flow
  • Thirteen languages, fully translated and included in the item — English, Bangla, Arabic, Hindi, Russian, Turkish, German, Chinese, Amharic, French, Greek, Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese, with right-to-left layout for Arabic. Nothing to buy and nobody to hire: see Languages.

What is not included

Online multiplayer, user accounts, leaderboards, in-app purchases, chat, and any form of real-money play are deliberately out of scope. The coin economy is virtual only — coins cannot be bought, cashed out or transferred.

Third-party services are not included in your purchase. Any cost, account, approval or policy obligation attached to a service outside this codebase is yours, and none of it is covered by the price of the item:

  • Google AdMob — real ads need your own AdMob account and your own ad unit IDs. Account approval, ad revenue, payment thresholds, policy compliance and any account suspension are between you and Google. See AdMob setup.
  • Google Play — publishing needs your own Play developer account, which carries a registration fee payable to Google.
  • Apple — an iOS release needs your own Apple Developer Program membership, an annual fee, and a Mac to build on. See iOS release build.
  • Privacy policy hosting — both stores require a publicly reachable URL, and arranging it is yours. See Your privacy policy.

The game itself has no server, no subscription and no running cost of its own. If you would rather ship without advertising, it reaches the game in only two screens — lib/features/game/match_stakes.dart and lib/features/daily_reward/daily_reward_screen.dart.

The robot opponent runs the same physics engine the player does, searching hundreds of candidate shots per turn in a background isolate. There is no scripted cheating, and no network call is ever made during a match.

Six steps to make it yours

Six steps from the unzipped archive to a build you can upload to a store under your own name. Each step links to the section with the detail, but the list stands on its own — nothing else in the project has to be touched to publish the game as yours.

  1. Install it and run it. With the Flutter SDK on your machine, from the project folder: 
    flutter pub get
flutter run
    The game builds and opens on any connected device or emulator, fully playable, with no account, key or configuration of any kind. See Requirements and Installation.
  2. Rename the app. Three files carry the display name: android:label in android/app/src/main/AndroidManifest.xml, CFBundleDisplayName in ios/Runner/Info.plist, and the title and names in web/index.html and web/manifest.json. See Rebranding.
  3. Change the application ID. The project ships as com.carromcircuit. In android/app/build.gradle.kts change both namespace and applicationId, move android/app/src/main/kotlin/com/carromcircuit/MainActivity.kt into a folder path that matches your new ID, update its package line, and set the bundle identifier for iOS in Xcode. Neither store lets you change this after the first upload, so choose it before you publish rather than after.
  4. Replace the launcher icon. Either drop your own artwork over the generated files, or edit the colours at the top of tool/generate_app_icons.py and re-run it: 
    python3 tool/generate_app_icons.py
    It writes every Android, iOS and web size from one drawing, including the Android adaptive layers and the Android 13 themed icon. See Rebranding.
  5. Put in your own AdMob IDs. The item ships with Google's public test IDs, so advertising works immediately and earns nobody anything. Replace the application ID in the Android manifest and the iOS Info.plist, replace the unit IDs in lib/services/ad_ids.dart, and set kReleaseAds = true. All of it is one short file and two lines of XML. See AdMob setup.
  6. Build a release. Create a signing key, fill in android/key.properties from the supplied example, then: 
    flutter build appbundle --release
    That is the file you upload to Google Play. See Android release build, and Store checklist for what else the store asks for.

Everything past those six steps is optional. The rules, the economy, the shop, the robot opponent and all thirteen languages work exactly as shipped, with no configuration at all. The rest of this document is there for when you want to change how something behaves.

Keeping the dependencies up to date

The versions in pubspec.yaml are the ones this release was built and tested against together. They are not frozen, and an app you intend to maintain for years should not stay on them forever — but upgrade deliberately rather than all at once:

  1. See what has moved. 
    flutter pub outdated
    The Resolvable column is what you can take without editing pubspec.yaml; Latest is what exists, and reaching it may mean a major version with breaking changes.
  2. Take the safe ones first. 
    flutter pub upgrade
    This stays inside the constraints already written in pubspec.yaml, so it picks up patch and minor releases only. This is the upgrade worth doing routinely.
  3. Then the major ones, one at a time. 
    flutter pub upgrade --major-versions
    Prefer editing one constraint by hand, re-running, and testing, over taking every major bump in a single step — when something breaks you want to know which package did it. Read the package's changelog on pub.dev first.
  4. Prove it still works, every time, before you commit: 
    flutter analyze
flutter test
    The test suite is the real safety net here: the physics, the rules engines, the AI and the economy are covered by unit tests that do not need a device, so a dependency that changes behaviour tends to show up as a failing test rather than as a bug a player finds.

Three things specific to this project that are worth knowing before you upgrade:

  • flutter_riverpod is held at ^3.3.2 on purpose. Newer releases have required a newer Dart SDK than the one this was built against. Upgrading Flutter first, then Riverpod, is the order that works.
  • intl is declared as any, which is deliberate. flutter_localizations pins it from the SDK to one exact version, and declaring a range of our own would only produce a conflict the next time you upgrade Flutter.
  • A major google_mobile_ads upgrade is rarely only a version bump. It tends to come with a minimum Android SDK or iOS deployment target, and sometimes a manifest or Podfile change. Read its changelog, then re-run the app on a real device and confirm an ad still loads — an ads SDK that fails quietly looks exactly like an empty network.

After any upgrade that touches iOS, re-run cd ios && pod install and commit the updated Podfile.lock along with pubspec.lock, so your own builds stay reproducible.

Connect a device or start an emulator, then:

Where the conventional Flutter folders are

This project groups code by layer rather than by widget type, because the game logic has to stay free of Flutter imports — that is what lets the robot opponent run the real rules and the real physics inside a background isolate, and what makes all of it unit testable. If you are looking for the folders a Flutter project more usually has:

models / entities lib/core/ — rules/, economy/ and physics/ hold the plain-Dart value types
screens / pages lib/features/, one folder per screen
widgets lib/shared/widgets/ for the reusable ones; a screen's own widgets sit beside it, and the in-game overlays are in lib/game/overlays/

State is managed with Riverpod, so nothing is passed down through widget trees by hand. Lists and grids that can grow are built lazily: the avatar picker's forty-nine tiles use GridView.builder, the twelve prize tables use PageView.builder, and the mode list uses ListView.separated. The settings, shop and daily-reward pages use a plain ListView, which is what Flutter recommends for a short and fixed set of children — those pages have a handful each, and a builder there would cost more than it saves.

Code quality gates

The project analyses more strictly than the Flutter defaults: implicit downcasts, inference that falls back to dynamic and raw generic types are all rejected, and a further set of lints is enabled in analysis_options.yaml. Both commands below should be clean before you ship a change:

flutter analyze
flutter test

One test is structural rather than behavioural: test/core_purity_test.dart fails if anything under lib/core/ imports Flutter, Flame or dart:ui. That boundary is what allows the rules and physics to run inside a background isolate for the robot's shot search, so it is worth keeping intact if you extend the game logic.

The physics engine's tests are mutation-verified. A deliberate fault is introduced into the engine one at a time — a sign flipped, a guard removed, a threshold moved — and the suite has to notice every one. That is a stronger claim than a passing suite: a green run proves the tests executed, not that they would catch a break. Three hundred mutations, all caught.

Mutation testing is how several of the rules documented here were found to be untested despite a green suite, and it is worth knowing about if you change the engine: a passing suite after a physics change does not mean the change is safe, only that nothing already asserted broke. If you alter lib/core/physics/ or lib/core/rules/, add a test that fails before your change and passes after it — that is the discipline the engine was built with, and the reason its behaviour is reproducible.

Two structural tests enforce boundaries rather than behaviour, and both are worth keeping intact: test/core_purity_test.dart, described above, and the determinism tests in test/physics/, which assert that the same shot produces byte-identical output. The robot's shot search depends on that: it replays thousands of candidate shots through the same engine, so a non-deterministic change would make the AI's choices unrepeatable as well as untestable.

Never run two at once, and never run --check beside a full sweep: a sweep keeps one file mutated while the suite runs, so anything else reading the source at that moment sees the mutated copy and reports patterns as stale when they are not.

The source is formatted with dart format, so dart format lib test should report nothing changed. Run it before committing rather than after, for the reason above.

Every mutation should be reported as caught. One reported as a gap means the suite would not have noticed that rule breaking — useful to run after you change the engine, and a good way to confirm your own additions are actually covered.

Gameplay settings

All gameplay numbers are collected in configuration files rather than scattered through the code, so you can tune the game without touching logic.

How the board is drawn

The board is painted with Canvas rather than loaded from an image, in lib/game/components/board_component.dart. Frame, surface, pockets, baselines, centre circles and arrows each have their own method, so a change stays local: edit _paintSurface to restyle the playing area, _paintPockets to restyle the holes, and so on. Replacing the whole look with your own artwork means replacing that one component.

Everything is expressed in board units and the camera is fixed to show exactly the board, so nothing in the game layer does screen-pixel arithmetic. That is why the board fits every screen size without breakpoints.

The board and the pieces are drawn once, not every frame. Neither picture changes while a match is played — the board is fixed and a coin only moves — so each is rasterised into an image by lib/game/paint_cache.dart and blitted from then on, at the scale the screen is actually drawing at. Profiling on a device put the saving at roughly 2 ms of GPU time per frame. You edit the painting methods exactly as before and the cache picks the change up; it rasterises your code rather than a copy of it. The one thing to know: anything drawn outside the cached box is cut off. The box is the board plus _shadowSpill in board_component.dart, and a piece's radius times kPieceSpill in piece_painter.dart, both sized for the blurred shadows that already spill past their shapes. If you add a glow or a larger shadow, widen the matching constant.

The coins and the striker are drawn by coin_component.dart and striker_component.dart, which share one paintPiece helper so every piece catches the light from the same direction. Neither component stores a position: each reads it from its body in the physics world every frame, so what you see can never disagree with what the engine is simulating.

The opening arrangement is built by lib/core/physics/formation.dart — queen on the centre spot, six coins touching her, twelve packed around those on a hexagonal lattice. Change the lattice there if you want a different starting layout; the colours alternate automatically.

Board dimensions

lib/game/config/board_config.dart. The board is modelled in logical units where one unit is one millimetre of a real carrom board: a 740 × 740 playing surface, 30 pocket radius, 15.5 coin radius and 26 striker radius. The Flame layer scales these to fit any screen, so changing them changes proportions, not the fit.

The pocket is deliberately larger than a real board’s, which is about 22.25 units by this scale and is where this project started. It was raised to 30 because the game was measured as too hard to score in: on a scattered board a perfectly aimed shot dropped only 46.7% of the time at the old size, against 66.7% at this one. The gain flattens above this — 27 already reaches 63.3% — so there is little left to buy by going further.

The striker is deliberately larger than a real one too — 26 against about 20.5 by this scale — and the two numbers belong together. At 20.5 the 30-unit pocket swallowed the striker about four times as often as the original board did (2.7% of shots across a sweep of every spot, angle and power, against 0.7%), which is a foul the player did not earn. Widening the striker brings that to 1.3% and, pleasingly, costs nothing in scoring: a bigger striker is a bigger hammer and finds the coin it is aimed at slightly more often. It must stay below kPocketRadius; a striker too fat for the hole could never be pocketed, and the Section 7.2 striker foul is a rule of the game. A sweep in test/physics/shot_feel_test.dart guards both ends of that.

Two things move with kPocketRadius besides the capture circle, which is why it is worth changing only here. BoardGeometry.hasCushionAt opens a gap of exactly this radius at each end of every cushion, and BoardComponent draws the holes at this size — so the holes on screen and the holes the physics uses cannot disagree. The two stay consistent at any value: a body inside the cushion gap is by definition within the capture circle by the time it reaches the wall plane, so widening the gap can never let a coin leave the board. A test in test/physics/shot_feel_test.dart pins the easing as a consequence rather than as a number, by firing a shot that misses the hole’s centre line by 26 units — more than the old radius, less than this one — and requiring it to drop.

Physics feel

Every physics constant lives in lib/game/config/physics_config.dart, as a single kCarromPhysics object handed to the engine. The engine itself holds no numbers, so this file is the only place to tune the game's feel. The values most worth experimenting with:

ConstantDefaultEffect of raising it
Min striker speed470 units/sA firmer gentlest shot; see the note below
Max striker speed2200 units/sHarder shots at 100% power
Rolling friction600 units/s²Coins stop sooner; a slower, heavier feel
Coin–coin restitution0.92Livelier, more energetic collisions
Coin–wall restitution0.75Stronger rebounds off the cushions
Pocket capture speed1800Fewer coins rattle out of pockets
Striker mass1.6Coins are the reference at 1.0; a heavier striker shifts coins further

The two striker speeds are the ends of the power meter, and the pair matters more than either on its own. Pull-back maps onto the range between them, not up from zero: a shot at 0% power leaves at minStrikerSpeed and one at 100% at maxStrikerSpeed. That floor is not decoration. Distance travelled goes as the square of speed, so with a map that started at zero the weakest shots could not carry the striker as far as the pack — the bottom fifth of the meter wasted a turn, and taming the top end made the dead band larger rather than smaller. If you raise maxStrikerSpeed, check that full power still looks like a break rather than a pinball table; if you lower minStrikerSpeed, check that the softest shot still reaches the coins. The test group in test/physics/shot_feel_test.dart checks both for you.

Pocket capture is worth understanding before you change it. A coin reaching a pocket below pocketCaptureSpeed drops in; a faster one skips across the hole, strikes the far lip and stays in play. Raising the value makes hard shots at the corners more reliable and the game easier; lowering it makes power risky and rewards touch. It is one of the few numbers here that changes how the game plays rather than how it feels.

Three further constants control the simulation rather than the feel, and are better left alone unless you know why you are changing them: fixedTimeStep (1/120 s), maxSubSteps (4) and maxSubStepTravelRatio (0.5). Together they decide how finely a fast shot is subdivided. A striker at full power crosses about 20 units per step, more than a coin's radius, so without sub-stepping it could pass straight through a coin.

The physics engine is deterministic by design — identical input always produces an identical result, which is what lets the robot simulate shots accurately. The opponent plays by copying the board, running simulateUntilRest on hundreds of candidate shots, and choosing one; the real board then has to reproduce that shot exactly. Never introduce randomness or frame-time dependence into the physics step, or the opponent's chosen shot will stop matching what the player sees.

Striker placement

Where the striker may sit is decided by BaselinePlacement in lib/core/rules/striker_placement.dart: the baseline span, the conversion between the slide bar's position and a point on the board, and whether a spot is clear of every coin. The robot uses the same class, so it can never aim from a spot a player could not have used.

A spot that overlaps a coin is shown in red rather than being corrected, because the player chose it. If you would rather the striker snapped to the closest legal spot instead, call nearestClearFraction and use what it returns.

How a shot feels

The shot gesture is tuned in lib/game/config/input_config.dart:

ConstantDefaultEffect of raising it
kFullPowerPull260A longer drag is needed for a hard shot, so aiming is finer but more effort
kCancelPull26More forgiving about abandoning a shot, but very gentle taps become impossible
kAimLineLength760A longer aim line, at every power, and a guide that marks a coin further ahead

Pull-back maps onto the striker's speed through PhysicsConfig.shotSpeed, evenly across the range between minStrikerSpeed and maxStrikerSpeed rather than up from zero. The distance a player feels in their thumb matches the speed they see on the board. Shortening kFullPowerPull makes the game feel twitchier, lengthening it makes fine shots easier and hard shots a stretch — on a small phone that trade-off is worth testing in your own hands before changing it.

The aim line does not work that way: it is kAimLineLength long whatever the power. It also sets how far ahead the aim guide looks, so raising it is not purely cosmetic once the guide is switched on. The line answers “where am I pointing”, and that question has the same answer however hard the shot is going to be — tying its length to power put the guide at its shortest for exactly the delicate shots that need it most. How hard the shot will be is said twice over already, by the colour of the line and by the meter beside the board, and neither of those costs a player their aim to read. The line is then clipped at the cushion it points at, so it never paints across the frame and its tip always marks the wall the striker is aimed at. Covered by test/game/aim_line_test.dart.

Rules and turn timer

lib/core/rules/ holds a RulesConfig object with the variant toggles: whether the queen may only be pocketed after one of your own coins, whether failing to touch any coin is a foul, the turn timer length (20 seconds by default) and the Scramble point values.

Every rule in this folder is written against one object, ShotResult, rather than against the board or the physics engine. A shot produces one: what fell and in what order, whether the striker went down, and which coin the striker touched first — a cushion first does not count, because a rebound shot is legal. The game builds one the moment the board stops moving and hands it to the rules engine.

The Tournament engine lives in lib/core/rules/tournament_rules.dart and the toggles it reads are in RulesConfig:

ToggleDefaultWhat it changes
queenRequiresOwnCoinFirsttrueOff, and the queen may be taken at any time
noCoinTouchedIsFoulfalseOn, and a shot that reaches no coin ends the turn
turnTimedtrueOff, and strokes are not timed at all
turnSeconds20How long a player has to shoot, when turnTimed
coinsPerPlayer9How many coins a seat must bank to win

Four points in Section 7.2 of the project spec can be read two ways, and the engine settles each one. Written out because houses genuinely differ, and because each is a one-line change:

  • Needing a coin of your own before the queen means before that shot — your first coin and the queen in a single stroke is too early.
  • Taking the queen keeps the table, so the cover can be made on the very next stroke.
  • Taking the queen too early is not itself turn-ending; she simply goes back to the centre.
  • Your last coin comes back if the queen is unsettled, whether she is on the board or waiting to be covered.

Each has a test whose name begins INTERPRETATION: in test/rules/tournament_rules_test.dart, so you can find and change any of them quickly.

The Scramble engine is beside it in lib/core/rules/scramble_rules.dart. Nobody owns a colour there: any coin can be pocketed by either player and scores for whoever sank it, so the game is a points race that ends when the board is clear. Its four values are also in RulesConfig:

ValueDefaultWhat it changes
scrambleWhitePoints20Points for a white coin
scrambleBlackPoints10Points for a black coin

scrambleStrikerPenalty10Points lost for pocketing the striker, never below zero

Scramble's spec is shorter and leaves more unsaid, so it settles four readings of its own, each again a one-line change with a matching INTERPRETATION: test in test/rules/scramble_rules_test.dart:

  • The queen may be taken at any time — queenRequiresOwnCoinFirst is a Tournament toggle and Scramble does not read it. Only the cover holds her.
  • Any coin covers her, either colour; but only the player who sank her can cover her, so she can sit unsettled while the opponent shoots.
  • Taking the queen keeps the table, so the cover can be made on the very next stroke.
  • The cover is waived once no coins are left on the board. Without that the game cannot end: she needs a coin to settle, none remains, and she would return to the board to be pocketed again forever.

A level score is a draw — GameState.isDraw — rather than a sudden-death round, which the spec makes optional.

Turns, the clock and the HUD

One game is a Match (lib/core/rules/match.dart): the mode, the rules engine it builds, the board as the rules see it, and the turn clock. It is the single authority on whose turn it is — the board follows it, never the other way round — so a whole game can be played out in a test with no screen and no physics. GameMode.tournament and GameMode.scramble each build their own engine and pass your RulesConfig straight through.

The clock is TurnTimer, driven by the game loop rather than by a wall clock. It is held while a shot is playing out, because a player is timed on deciding and aiming, not on watching the coins run, and every stroke gets a fresh allowance — including the next one in a visit the player has kept, since holding one clock across a run of pocketed coins would punish them for shooting well. Change RulesConfig.turnSeconds for the length and TurnTimer.urgentSeconds for how long the final warning lasts; the ring around the active avatar follows both.

A match can also have no clock at all, which the player controls from the Turn timer switch in Settings and which RulesConfig.turnTimed sets. An untimed match has Match.timer == null rather than a timer nobody starts — so there is nothing to read a time out of, no ring is drawn round the active avatar, and the pause panel does not claim to have stopped a clock. If you build a screen that reads the clock, ask Match.isTimed first.

Both engines return instructions rather than positions: a TurnOutcome says only that a coin must come back, never which one or where. CentrePlacement (lib/core/rules/centre_placement.dart) is the other half, and it answers the Section 7.2 phrase "the centre, or the nearest free spot" by ringing outwards from the centre spot until it finds room that is on the surface, clear of every coin and off the pocket mouths. The robot will use the same code, because a candidate shot has to be simulated from the position a returned coin would really take.

The HUD is Flutter widgets, not Flame components: a panel at each end of the screen so each seat's own information is nearest them. HudPanel watches HudViews — three notifiers rather than one, because the state changes once a stroke, the clock every frame and the input gate twice a stroke, and bundling them would repaint both panels sixty times a second to animate one ring. Tournament gives each seat a tray of its own colour, as a real board does; Scramble has a single shared tray of what has come off the board, because nobody owns a colour there and a tray beside a Scramble player would claim an ownership the rules do not recognise. Avatars are Canvas-drawn (lib/shared/widgets/player_avatar.dart): either initials on a coloured disc, which is what the sixteen robot avatars of the matchmaking screen use, or one of the forty-eight drawn symbols a player can choose for themselves. See the player's name and avatar.

Modes, seats and Pass & Play

The four modes of the item description are really two axes. Tournament and Scramble name the rules; Pass & Play and Solo Match name the opponent. MatchSetup (lib/core/rules/match_setup.dart) carries one of each, so every combination is playable and the robot does not need a fifth and sixth mode invented for it. It is what the board takes as its Routes.game route argument:

Navigator.pushNamed(
  context,
  Routes.game,
  arguments: const MatchSetup(rules: GameMode.scramble),
);

Pushing the route without an argument falls back to two players at a Tournament board. On the mode-select screen, Tournament and Scramble go straight in; the Pass & Play card asks which rules first.

In a two-player game the whole board turns half a turn for the second seat, and the two player panels swap with it, so whoever is holding the phone always shoots from the bottom of the screen with the slide bar directly below their own baseline. A brief "Player 2's turn" notice fades in and out over the board as the table passes; the turn clock keeps running underneath it, so nothing a player has to tap costs them their own time.

The turn is done in the Flutter layer with AnimatedRotation, not by rotating Flame's camera. A Transform transforms hit tests as well as painting, so the catapult pull keeps meaning what the player sees it mean with no coordinate arithmetic anywhere: pulling down the screen still fires up the screen even though "down the screen" is now "up the board". The one thing that does need converting is the slide bar, which sits outside the rotated board — barFraction in lib/game/input/bar_mapping.dart mirrors it, so the knob always follows the thumb.

Turning the board never happens against the robot, which is not holding anything, and a player can switch it off in Settings if they play with the phone flat between them.

This matters if you write your own variant. Because a rule only ever reads a ShotResult, you can test one without a physics world, and the robot scores a candidate shot with the same code that scores a real one — so the opponent can never play by rules the player is not held to. Build a ShotResult by hand in a test and assert what your rule does with it; see test/rules/shot_result_test.dart.

The robot's shot search

The robot plays by searching, not by rules of thumb. It generates shots that are aimed at a particular coin towards a particular pocket, plays each one out on a private copy of the board using the same physics engine the player's shots use, hands the result to the same rules engine, scores it and ranks the lot. Two consequences worth knowing before you change anything: the opponent can never play by rules the player is not held to, and because the engine is deterministic the shot it picks plays out on screen exactly as it did in the search.

Everything is pure Dart in lib/core/ai/, so you can test it without a screen:

FileWhat it is
shot_candidate.dartOne shot: a spot on the baseline, an angle, a power. The only shot value object in the project.
aim_geometry.dartThe arithmetic. Where the striker must be at contact to send a coin to a pocket, whether a path is clear, and where to strike a cushion for a rebound.
candidate_generator.dartFinds the aims on a board, prunes the hopeless ones and expands the rest into shots at each power.
shot_simulator.dartPlays a candidate out to a standstill on a copy of the board, reusing one world so the search is affordable.
shot_evaluator.dartTurns a finished shot into a number, one implementation per mode.
shot_search.dartTies it together and ranks the results. ShotSearch.inBackground runs the whole thing on a background isolate so the board keeps its frame rate while the robot thinks.

There are two things to tune, and they are independent. How wide the robot looks is CandidatePlan in lib/core/ai/candidate_plan.dart: spots along the baseline, power levels, the spread either side of a dead-on aim, and how many aims survive pruning. What the robot thinks a shot is worth is AiWeights in lib/core/ai/ai_weights.dart: a coin, the queen, the cover, the striker foul, an opponent coin, and how much it cares about leaving coins near pockets. A robot that gambles the striker too readily or chases the queen too hard is tuned there, not in the search.

One number in the generator is worth explaining, because it looks like a bug and is not. A path counts as clear when an obstacle leaves 0.7 of the room the moving body really needs, rather than all of it (CandidatePlan.grazeClearance). Carrom is played on a packed board: nineteen coins start out touching, a coin leaving a cluster shoves its neighbours aside, and the striker cannot reach a coin in a cluster without grazing the ones beside it. Insisting on the full clearance rejects every shot there is. The error is deliberately on the permissive side — a shot that will not work is thrown out by the simulator a millisecond later, whereas a shot wrongly refused is never tried at all.

Robot difficulty

lib/core/ai/difficulty.dart holds the table and lib/core/ai/robot_player.dart plays to it. A difficulty is three separate ways to be weak, and they give the robot quite different characters:

DifficultyCandidatesPicks fromAngle noisePower noise
Easy~150top 40%±4°±15%
Medium~400top 10%±1.8°±8%
Hard~900the best±0.6°±3%

A narrow search means the robot never sees the good shot — the beginner who misses the rebound. A loose pick means it sees the good shot and plays a worse one — the player with no judgement. Noise means it picks the right shot and mis-hits it — the player whose hands are not steady. Easy is weak in all three ways, because a robot weak in only one is recognisably a machine with a handicap. Each is a separate field on RobotProfile, so you can make an opponent that sees everything and still fumbles it, or one with a steady hand and no sense.

Each tier's candidate count is the exact product of its own CandidatePlan rather than a quota applied afterwards, so widening a tier means looking at more aims — which is what actually makes the robot see more of the board.

The seed

RobotPlayer takes a seed, and never creates one of its own. Everything about a stroke — which shot it picks, how far it misses, how long it appears to think — is a pure function of the seed, the stroke number and the search result. The same seed replays the same game, which is what makes a robot's turn reproducible in a test and in a bug report.

If you add a feature where two devices watch the same game, pass the seed between them and both will see the robot play identically. That only works because each stroke draws from its own generator rather than from one long stream; a stroke can therefore be recomputed on its own, without replaying the ones before it.

Playing the robot

Solo Match asks two questions — which rules, then how strong a robot — and then deals the board. The player shoots from the near baseline and the robot from the far one, as Section 7.1 seats them.

What the player watches is deliberate. The robot slides the striker into the spot it chose and then pulls back to the power it chose, because a shot that appeared out of nowhere would be indistinguishable from the game cheating: the slide says where it is shooting from and the pull says how hard, which is everything you would read from an opponent's hands at a real board. Both are drawn by exactly the code that draws the player's own shot — AimState.pulled turns a shot back into the drag that would have produced it — so the two can never show different things.

The timings live in lib/game/config/robot_config.dart: the slide, the pull, and a short beat at full stretch before it lets go. The thinking pause itself is Section 8's, on RobotProfile.

The search and the thinking pause run together. The robot starts searching the moment the table passes to it and the pause is measured from that same moment, so a search that takes a second eats into a pause that was going to be a second and a half rather than adding to it. This matters on a phone, where a Hard search is the slowest thing the game does. If you widen CandidatePlan past the pause, the robot simply plays as soon as it is ready.

The robot is not timed. The turn clock is for a person deciding; a robot cannot stall, and a countdown it could lose to its own search would be a foul nobody could have prevented. Its avatar shows a sweep while it is working instead of a ring.

Pausing, the result and a rematch

The pause button is the only way out of a game, so leaving is always deliberate rather than a mis-tap on a back arrow — the Android back gesture opens the pause menu too. Pausing stops the Flame engine, which stops the turn clock with it: the clock is only ever advanced from the game's update, so there is no second thing to remember to pause. The same is true when the player takes a call or switches apps, because Flame pauses the engine when the app is backgrounded, so nobody loses a turn to a notification.

The result panel shows as soon as the rules declare the game over. In Tournament it shows the board points of the spec — one per coin the loser left, plus three for the queen if the winner covered her, display only — and in Scramble the two final scores. Both are produced by resultSummary in lib/game/overlays/result_overlay.dart, a pure function, so the wording and the arithmetic are tested without building a widget. Rematch lays the board out again from the opening arrangement without leaving the screen; the pause menu offers the same thing as "Start again".

Every sentence those two show is in one file, lib/shared/utils/game_strings.dart. resultSummary and turnMessage decide which line to show and never build one: they are handed a GameStrings and ask it. If you want "Ana wins" to read differently, or the middle dot between a foul and the next player to be something else, change it there and both the board and the result panel follow.

The reason it is shaped that way is translation. Each method returns a whole sentence with its values as arguments, never a fragment glued to another fragment, because word order is a property of a language — a line built as '$foul · $name to play' can be translated into English and only approximated into anything else. test/game/game_strings_test.dart holds every method to the rule that matters here: whatever values a sentence is given have to appear in what it returns, so a wording that quietly loses a player's name fails a test. It asserts presence rather than a count, deliberately, because a language may well mention a number once where English mentions it twice.

Leaving a local game costs nothing, so neither menu warns before it. That changes when the prize tables arrive: an entry fee has been paid by then, and quitting is a loss.

Settings

Settings are a value object in lib/core/settings/game_settings.dart with a JSON round trip, stored under one key by StorageService and exposed through settingsProvider in lib/services/settings_provider.dart — the same pattern as the wallet. A damaged entry falls back to the defaults rather than throwing, so a corrupt preference can never stop the game opening.

Seven settings: the language, turning the board round, Sound, Vibration, the aim guide, the turn timer and the time allowed for each shot. There is no music toggle, because there is no music track — a stored field nothing reads is a field that quietly rots, so it goes in when there is something for it to control. Adding one means a field on GameSettings, a setter on SettingsNotifier and a row on the settings screen.

The player's name and avatar

The first row of Settings opens a Profile screen (lib/features/profile/profile_screen.dart) where a player types a name and taps a face. Both are optional: a player who never opens it is called Player 1 and wears their own initials, which is exactly what the game showed before the screen existed.

The model is PlayerProfile (lib/core/profile/player_profile.dart) — a name and an avatar index, stored under the profile key with the same JSON round trip as the wallet. Two details are worth knowing before you change anything:

  • An unset name is the empty string, not 'Player 1'. The display fallback lives in the interface layer as kDefaultPlayerName (lib/shared/utils/player_display.dart), because lib/core/ must not hold a user-facing English sentence. Change the name a player is called by default there, in one place.
  • The name is stored on every keystroke, not on submit. A player who types a name and taps Back has said what they want to be called. Names are trimmed, internal whitespace is collapsed, and the length is capped at 20 code points rather than code units, so an emoji costs one and a surrogate pair is never cut in half.

There are forty-nine avatars: entry 0 is the player's initials, and entries 1 to 48 are a drawn symbol on a coloured disc. Forty-eight is arithmetic rather than taste. An avatar is a pair taken from two lists of deliberately different lengths — twelve symbols in AvatarMotif and the sixteen colours AvatarPalette already had for the robots — so the map from an index to the pair repeats only after lcm(12, 16) = 48:

// lib/shared/widgets/avatar_catalogue.dart
symbol = m % 12        // AvatarMotif.count
colour = m % 16        // AvatarPalette.colours.length
static const int motifCount = 48;   // lcm of the two

If you add or remove a symbol or a colour, recompute motifCount. It is the least common multiple of the two list lengths, and a larger number silently ships two avatars that look identical. test/profile/avatar_catalogue_test.dart recomputes that least common multiple from the two lists and asserts motifCount equals it, so getting it wrong fails a test rather than reaching a player.

Callers never ask for a colour and a symbol separately: AvatarCatalogue.lookFor returns one AvatarLook, so the HUD, the settings row and the picker cannot each answer half the question. The fallback disc colour is passed in rather than chosen by the catalogue, because it belongs to the seat — maple for the near player, walnut for the far one.

One profile, not one per seat. In Pass & Play the second seat keeps its default name and initials, and the robot keeps AI. A device has one owner. If you want a second stored identity, GameLayout already takes the near seat's initials as a parameter, so the change is confined to the screen that builds it.

There is deliberately no avatar from a photo on the device. It was planned and dropped: a gallery picker costs a package with native code on both platforms, an iOS photo-library usage string, a disclosure in your privacy policy and an extra Play Data safety declaration — for a 46-pixel disc that already has forty-nine faces. If you add one anyway, those four obligations are yours to meet.

Economy & prize tables

Coins are virtual, stored on the device, and have no real-world value. New players start with 1,000.

Prize tables

lib/core/economy/prize_tables.dart defines the 12 tables — number, entry cost, prize, game mode and robot difficulty. Add, remove or reprice tables by editing that list. Tables unlock progressively: a player unlocks the next one by winning at the previous table, or by holding at least three times its entry cost.

The names are not in that file. A table name is text a player reads, so it lives in lib/l10n/app_en.arb under the keys tableQualifier … tableHallOfFame, and prizeTableNames in lib/shared/utils/core_labels.dart is the list that maps a table's number to its key. Rename a table by editing the ARB; add a table by adding a key, the matching entry to that list, and a translation to every finished .arb. A table with no name throws rather than guessing, and test/app/core_labels_test.dart fails first, naming the number.

The stock ladder runs Qualifier, Club Night, Town League, District Cup, City Open, Regional Open, National Open, Invitational, Masters Table, Grand Prix, Champion’s Table, Hall of Fame. The names are meant to read as one season, so a player can tell roughly how far up they are from the name alone. If you rebrand, keeping that sense of progression matters more than the particular words.

Entries, prizes and walking out

The entry is taken when the match starts, not when the table is chosen — a player who backs out of matchmaking has paid nothing. A win pays the prize and records the table, which opens the one above it. A loss pays nothing: the entry is already gone.

Quitting mid-match is a loss, as Section 9.2 requires, and the pause menu says so before it lets the player go. A rematch is a new match and pays a second entry; a player who cannot afford one is told rather than given a free game.

A drawn game — possible in Scramble — returns the entry. The spec is silent on it, and a draw is explicitly not a loss, so keeping the entry for a game nobody won would be the house taking coins for nothing. That is one line in PrizeSettlement.settle (lib/core/economy/match_settlement.dart) if you play it differently.

All of the arithmetic is in that one file and is pure, so you can change what a table pays without going anywhere near the board. PrizePurse (lib/features/game/prize_purse.dart) is the thin layer that commits a settlement to the wallet and the ladder, and it guarantees a match settles only once.

Starting balance and saved data

A new player begins with 1,000 coins. Change that one constant to change the whole economy's starting point:

// lib/core/economy/wallet.dart
static const int startingBalance = 1000;
static const int lowBalanceThreshold = 50;

Everything the game remembers — wallet, settings, the player's own name and avatar, owned equipment, prize-table progress and daily-reward streak — is stored on the device through shared_preferences, one JSON object per feature under the keys listed in StorageKeys (lib/services/storage_service.dart). Nothing is sent anywhere, and there is no account to sign in to.

Clearing the app's data from Android settings, or reinstalling, resets a player to the starting balance. That is intentional: there is no server to restore from. If you want progress to survive a reinstall you would need to add your own cloud save, which is outside the scope of this item.

A day is a calendar date, not twenty-four hours. A player who collects at five to midnight and comes back after breakfast has come back the next day, and gets the next day's reward. A missed day puts the run back to day one, and the screen says so rather than leaving the player to notice the calendar has reset. Finishing the run starts it again at day one rather than paying 1,000 for ever.

Device clocks. Both features read the clock through clockProvider (lib/services/clock_provider.dart) rather than calling DateTime.now directly, which is what lets the tests sit on any date they like. A stored time in the future — the mark of a device clock that was wrong and has since been corrected — is treated as a break in the run and a cooldown that has expired, never as a reason to refuse. A cooldown held on the device cannot be enforced against a clock the player controls in any case, and the outcome worth avoiding is an honest player locked out of the only coins they can reach.

A player who is short of coins and waiting out the cooldown is also offered the refill immediately in exchange for a rewarded video. The video buys the wait, not a second helping: the four hours restart exactly as they do for a free refill, so five videos cannot become five refills. See the AdMob section for the video itself.

Opponent names and avatars

The matchmaking screen rolls through the roster in lib/core/economy/robot_names.dart — 66 robots across Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka, Nepal and Pakistan. Edit the per-country name lists to suit your market; the avatar numbering is worked out from the list, so adding or removing names needs nothing else changed.

A robot is a name, a country and one of sixteen faces. The face is fixed to the name rather than drawn at random, so a player who meets the same opponent twice meets the same one. Faces are a colour and an initial, drawn rather than loaded from assets — the sixteen colours are in lib/shared/widgets/avatar_palette.dart, and flags are emoji rather than images, so the whole roster costs nothing to ship.

Which opponent appears, and every shot that opponent will mis-hit, both come from a single seed drawn when matchmaking starts. That means a match is described completely by its table and its seed, which is what makes one reproducible in a bug report.

Climbing the ladder

A table opens when the player has won at the table below it, or when they are holding three times its entry. Either is enough. The first table is always open, so a player who has lost everything can still get back in.

Those are two different questions from can this player pay the entry right now, and the card says so in different words: a shut table tells you what would open it, and an open one you cannot afford tells you what it costs. Keep that distinction if you edit the wording — telling a player to go and win somewhere else when all they need is more coins sends them off for nothing.

Which tables have been won is stored under StorageKeys.prizeTables. Note that the wealth route reads the most the wallet has ever held, not today's balance: a table opened by a balance stays open once that balance is spent, so paying an entry can never shut the table you are sitting at. The entry itself still has to be affordable, which is what keeps a broke player out of the Hall of Fame.

Store policy. Because entry costs and prizes could be mistaken for gambling, the interface uses the words entry and prize throughout, never bet or stake money, and coins can never be purchased. Keep that language if you edit the strings.

Equipment shop

Fifteen items across three categories, bought with coins and purely cosmetic. One item is in play in each category at all times, and the cheapest in each is free, so a new player always has a striker, a board and a set of coins.

Everything here is built: the catalogue, the buy and equip flow, persistence, and the skins themselves. What a player has equipped is what they play on — the board, its frame and markings, the striker, the coins on the board and the coins stacked in the trays beside each seat.

Adding, removing or re-pricing an item

It takes three steps, because what the item is and what it is called live apart. First, add an entry to the right list in ShopCatalog:

EquipmentItem(
  id: 'striker_onyx',                  // stable, never shown to a player
  category: EquipmentCategory.striker,
  price: 9000,
)

Second, add its name and description to lib/l10n/app_en.arb — and to every finished translation — then run flutter gen-l10n:

"itemStrikerOnyx": "Onyx",
"itemStrikerOnyxDescription": "Black glass, gold flecked.",

Third, map the id to those keys in lib/shared/utils/core_labels.dart:

'striker_onyx': l10n.itemStrikerOnyx,            // in _equipmentNames
'striker_onyx': l10n.itemStrikerOnyxDescription, // in _equipmentDescriptions

Miss the third step and the shop throws an ArgumentError naming the id rather than drawing a nameless card — and test/app/core_labels_test.dart fails before you ever see it.

Four rules the tests enforce, so you will hear about it if one is broken:

  • The id is what storage remembers, and it is deliberately separate from the name. Rename or translate an item freely in the ARB; changing its id is what orphans a purchase a player has already paid for.
  • No two items in a category may share a name, because there is nothing else on a card to tell them apart but the price.
  • Exactly one free item per category. It is what a new player starts with and what an unreadable saved choice falls back to, so a category of entirely paid items would leave a player with no board to play on.
  • Prices rise within a category, so the shop reads left to right as a ladder.

Removing an item is safe. A saved id the catalogue no longer recognises is kept in storage but stops being used, and the player falls back to the free item of that category — so a removal costs a skin, not a crash, and putting the item back restores the purchase.

A new item also needs a palette, or it will be drawn exactly like the free item of its category. See How an item looks below. A test fails if any item in the catalogue has no palette of its own, so you will not ship a 9,000-coin striker that looks like the free one by accident.

How an item looks

Colours are not held by the drawing code. Each Canvas component is handed a palette, which is what makes a skin a value you can edit rather than a change spread across ten files. The palettes live in lib/game/skins/, one file per category:

FileTypeWhat it colours
board_skin.dartBoardSkin The frame, the playing surface and its grain, the markings, the pocket rims and holes, and the background the board sits on
striker_skin.dartStrikerSkin The striker's face, rim and target ring
coin_skin.dartCoinSkin The white, black and queen coins — face and rim for each
equipment_skins.dartEquipmentSkins The three above together. EquipmentSkins.forInventory() turns what the player has equipped into the palettes a game is played with

A palette is matched to an item by its id, so to add one, add a const beside the others and list it in that file's all:

static const StrikerSkin onyx = StrikerSkin(
  id: 'striker_onyx',          // the catalogue id, exactly
  face: Color(0xFF2B2B33),
  edge: Color(0xFF0D0D11),
  ring: Color(0xFFD8B24A),
);

Two tests guard the pairing in both directions: every item the shop sells must have a palette carrying its own id, and no palette may belong to an id the shop does not sell — the second is how a rename done on only one side gets caught. Two more check a skin cannot make the board unreadable: the markings must contrast with both ends of the surface gradient, and a coin set must keep a strong light-dark split. Edit the colours freely; if a board's markings vanish into its surface, the suite says so by name.

The same palettes draw the swatch on each shop card, through the same painting code as the board, so the window and the board cannot drift apart.

The optional striker perk

Section 9.5 of the design allows one non-cosmetic perk: a longer aim guide on premium strikers. It ships switched off, so the shop sells nothing but looks — which is what the item description claims, and what a store reviewer will read. The toggle is a single constant in lib/game/config/input_config.dart:

const bool kStrikerPerkEnabled = false;

Each striker already carries its own aimGuideScale, rising from 1.0 on the free Rookie to 1.3 on Eclipse, so switching the constant to true gives you a working ladder with no other edit. Note that it changes how the game plays, not just how it looks: the longer guide still stops at the cushion, but a paying player sees further than a new one — both along the aim line and in how far ahead the aim guide will mark a coin.

Buying and equipping

A card shows one of four states: equipped, owned with an Equip button, buyable with the price on the button, or unaffordable with a note saying how many coins are needed. The last two are kept apart on purpose: an unaffordable item and an unchosen one are different problems with different answers.

Buying asks for confirmation first, naming the item, the price and the balance it will leave — a purchase is the one thing in the menus that spends coins and cannot be undone. Buying also equips, since a player who has just paid for a striker wants to use it; the item it replaced stays owned, so going back is one tap.

ShopNotifier.buy in lib/services/shop_provider.dart is the only code that spends coins here. It checks ownership and the balance itself and returns what it spent, so a card that is a frame out of date cannot charge twice.

What is stored

One JSON blob under the shop key (StorageKeys.shop), holding the ids bought and the id chosen in each category:

{"owned":["striker_slate","board_walnut"],
 "equipped":{"striker":"striker_slate","board":"board_walnut"}}

Free items are not listed — they are owned by definition. A corrupt or missing blob costs the player their skins and nothing else: they are put back on the free items and the game opens normally.

AdMob setup

The project ships with Google's official test ad unit IDs so you can run it immediately without an AdMob account. You must switch to your own IDs before publishing.

Advertising is the only external service this game talks to. It uses the Google Mobile Ads SDK for Flutter (the google_mobile_ads package), together with Google's User Messaging Platform for consent, which ships inside that same package. There is no other API, no backend of ours, and no network call anywhere else in the game.

Every AdMob ID in this package is a Google test value

Nothing in this archive carries an author-owned AdMob identity. You can confirm it in five places before you change anything, and you should — a copy of somebody else's publisher ID would earn them money from your players, silently:

    An AdMob app is per platform, so shipping to both stores means two AdMob apps and four ad units in total.     google.com, pub-XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX, DIRECT, f08c47fec0942fa0

    Your pub- number is the middle of your application ID: ca-app-pub-XXXX~YYYY becomes pub-XXXX. AdMob crawls the file about once a day, so the status stays Not found for a while after you upload it. Ads serve either way — an unverified app only loses bid value.

An application ID is not an ad unit ID. The application ID that goes in the manifest and the Info.plist is ca-app-pub-…~NNNN, with a tilde. An ad unit ID is ca-app-pub-…/NNNN, with a slash. Do not delete the application ID meta-data. The Google Mobile Ads SDK reads it from a ContentProvider during process start-up, before any Dart code runs. If the entry is missing or malformed the app crashes the instant it is opened, with IllegalStateException: Missing application ID. To strip ads out of the project entirely, remove the google_mobile_ads dependency as well, not just the manifest entry.

How an ad is loaded

Three files, in layers. lib/services/ad_ids.dart holds the unit IDs and the kReleaseAds flag. lib/services/ads_service.dart holds an AdSlot per unit, which keeps one ad loaded, retries a failed request and fetches the next the moment one is spent. lib/services/admob_loader.dart is the only file in the project that imports google_mobile_ads: everything above it works through a small FullScreenAd interface, which is what lets the loading rules be unit tested without an AdMob account.

Two constants on AdSlot are worth knowing about if you want to change how hard the app tries:

ConstantDefaultWhat it does
maxAttempts3 How many times one preload asks before giving up. It gives up rather than retrying for ever, so a player with no signal is not paying for a request every few seconds all session. The next preload starts a fresh set of attempts.
firstRetryDelay4 seconds The wait before the first retry, doubling after that — 4s, then 8s.

Nothing is fetched at start-up. An ad requested and never shown counts against the match rate AdMob pays on, and most sessions never ask for a rewarded video, so each slot is loaded by the screen that is about to need it.

When no ad is loaded, AdSlot.show() returns false rather than throwing or waiting, and the caller shows the "Video not available, try again later" message. A false always means the player saw nothing, so a rewarded video that returns false has earned nothing.

Where ads appear

  • Interstitial — only after a match against the robot, at most once every two matches and never less than three minutes apart. Never during play, and never after a local 2-player game.
  • Rewarded video — only when the player taps to watch one, always behind a confirmation dialog, with coins granted strictly in the reward callback.

The interstitial caps

Two limits, and both must be satisfied before an ad appears. They live in lib/core/ads/interstitial_cap.dart:

ConstantDefaultWhat it does
matchesBetweenAds2 Robot matches that must finish between two ads. A new player therefore finishes two matches before seeing anything.
minimumGap3 minutes The shortest time allowed between two ads, whatever the match count says. This is what stops a run of very short games being interrupted twice in a minute.

Both are stored, under the interstitial key, so closing the app is neither a way to be shown an ad sooner nor a way to avoid one.

An ad appears at a break — when the player leaves a finished match, or asks for a rematch — and never while a game is on screen. A match the player abandoned part-way is not counted and is never followed by an ad: quitting is scored as a loss, but it is not a match anybody sat through. A local two-player game neither counts nor shows, as Section 10 requires.

If you want ads to be rarer, raise either constant; the pair are independent and the stricter one wins. Setting matchesBetweenAds to a very large number is the practical way to turn interstitials off without removing the code.

The rewarded video

A video is worth 250 coins, and a player may watch five a day — 1,250 coins, which is worth comparing against the prize-table entries: a full day of videos buys a seat at the City Open and no more. A player can keep themselves at the board with videos; they cannot climb the ladder with them. Both numbers are in lib/core/ads/rewarded_allowance.dart:

ConstantDefaultWhat it does
RewardedVideo.coins250What one video pays
RewardedVideo.dailyLimit5How many may be watched in a day

A day is a calendar day, the same reading the daily reward streak takes and deliberately the same function: both are "once a day" rules on the same screen, and a player whose streak had rolled over while their video allowance had not would be looking at two different answers to what day it is.

A video is always behind a confirmation — "Watch a short video for 250 coins?" — and only ever starts from a tap that says so. Coins are credited inside the SDK's reward callback and nowhere else, so a video closed half way through pays nothing. Such a video also costs the player none of their five: charging an allowance for an ad that paid nothing would be taking something in exchange for nothing.

Videos are offered in two places, both on the coin screen:

  • 250 coins, any time, five a day.
  • The out-of-coins refill, immediately. A player who is short of coins and waiting out the four-hour cooldown can watch a video and have the 500 now. The video buys the wait, not a second helping: the cooldown restarts exactly as it does for a free refill, so five videos cannot become five refills. Offered only while both halves of the Section 9.4 rule apply — a low balance and a cooldown still running.

The free refill is listed above the video on that screen, which is not the order the specification lists them in. A player who is out of coins should be shown the thing that costs them nothing before the thing that costs them thirty seconds.

When nothing is loaded the player is told "Video not available, try again later." and nothing is charged. If you want videos to be rarer or worth more, change the two constants above; nothing else reads those numbers, and the copy in the panels and the confirmation is assembled from them.

Consent in GDPR regions

The app uses Google's User Messaging Platform, which ships inside google_mobile_ads — there is no extra dependency. The flow is in lib/services/consent_service.dart and you should not need to change it.

One step is yours and the project cannot do it for you: configure a GDPR message in your own AdMob account under Privacy & messaging. Until you do, UMP has no form to show, and players in the EEA will see no ads at all — which is the correct behaviour, not a bug.

Consent is gathered the first time an ad is requested, not at launch, and no ad is ever requested until the SDK says consent allows it. That check is a parameter of AdSlot (AdGate) rather than something buried in the plugin wrapper, so it is covered by tests: a refused gate means no request, is not retried, and a gate that throws is read as a refusal rather than as permission.

Note the apparent oddity that MobileAds.initialize() is still called from main.dart, before consent. That is deliberate. Initialising the SDK does not request an ad, and what Google's EU consent policy governs is the request. If your own legal advice is stricter, move that call into the consent flow: it is idempotent and awaited by every request, so the only consequence is that the first ad of a session takes a moment longer to arrive.

Where a region requires it, the settings screen grows an Advertising choices row that reopens Google's form, so a player can change their mind. It is hidden everywhere else, because a privacy control that does nothing for the person looking at it is worse than no control.

Seeing the consent form outside Europe

  1. Run the app and open the coin screen, or play a match against the robot, so that an ad is requested. Nothing is logged before that, because that is when consent is gathered.
  2. In the device log, under the tag UserMessagingPlatform, find Use new ConsentDebugSettings.Builder().addTestDeviceHashedId("…").
  3. Put that string into kConsentTestDeviceIds in lib/services/consent_service.dart, and set kConsentDebugGeography to DebugGeography.debugGeographyEea.
  4. Reinstall. That device now behaves as though it were in the EEA. The setting affects listed devices only, so it is harmless to leave in place — but clear it before a release build.

Remove ads

It is a sentence, not a control: there is no in-app purchase code in the project, and a greyed-out switch that can never be switched is worse than an honest note. If you do not intend to add the purchase, delete that one ComingSoonPanel from lib/features/settings/settings_screen.dart.

Languages

The game is localised with Flutter's own tooling — flutter_localizations from the SDK and gen-l10n — so there is no third-party localisation package to learn and no build step beyond the one flutter pub get already runs.

Thirteen languages ship: English, Bangla, Arabic, Hindi, Russian, Turkish, German, Chinese, Amharic, French, Greek, Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese. 211 sentences each. Nothing is bundled to support them — the platform's own fonts render Bengali, Devanagari, Arabic, Cyrillic, Greek, Han and Ethiopic on any current phone, which is why assets/fonts/ is empty.

Read lib/l10n/GLOSSARY.md before editing any translation. Thirteen words carry the game — striker, coin, queen, cover, baseline, foul and the rest — and a translator who renders cover as "lid" writes a sentence that reads perfectly and means nothing to a carrom player. The glossary names all thirteen in every language, and test/app/glossary_test.dart holds the translations to it: a term the glossary states and no sentence uses is a failing test.

Two of those words are not a matter of taste. Entry and prize must never become a stake, a wager or a bet in any language: the coins are virtual and cannot be bought, cashed out or transferred, and a store reviewer reads the translation too. The same test refuses a known betting word anywhere in any ARB file.

Adding a language

Six steps. Steps 2 to 5 are each caught by a test if you skip them, and the test says which — that was measured by adding a language the lazy way and reading what failed, rather than written from memory. Step 1 is checked for its mechanics only: nothing can tell whether you actually translated the words. Step 6 is the one to be careful of, and the warning below says why. Japanese, as an example:

  1. Copy the template and translate the values. Keep every key and every {placeholder} exactly as it is; the key is what the code asks for and the placeholder is where the number or the name goes. 
    cp lib/l10n/app_en.arb lib/l10n/app_ja.arb
    Metadata — the @key blocks describing each string — lives only in the template, so you can delete it all from your copy. Keep "@@locale" and change it to your language's code.
  2. Read the glossary first, and add your language to it. lib/l10n/GLOSSARY.md names thirteen carrom terms. Decide them once, then translate with them; it is far cheaper than discovering halfway through that cover has been rendered three different ways. Add a section for your language naming all thirteen.
  3. Name the language in its own language. Add it to kLanguageNames in lib/shared/utils/locale_choice.dart — 'ja': '日本語', not "Japanese". A picker that lists languages in the language you are already reading is no use to the person who needs it.
  4. Declare which script it is written in. Add it to _script in test/app/glossary_test.dart — 'ja': 'Han' — and add the range to _scripts if the alphabet is not already there. That test holds every string in a language to its own alphabet, which is the only thing that can catch a character from the wrong one: a Cyrillic о inside a Latin word renders identically and would otherwise ship.
  5. Hold it complete. Add 'app_ja.arb' to the complete set in test/app/localisation_test.dart once it carries every key. That set is what holds a finished translation to the template for ever; a language left out of it can quietly lose a sentence later and nothing will say so.
  6. Regenerate and run the tests. 
    flutter gen-l10n     # or just: flutter pub get
flutter test

Nothing else has a list to keep up to date. The picker and supportedLocales are both derived from the files that exist.

Regenerate after editing an ARB. The generated Dart in lib/l10n/ is what the app actually reads, so an edited .arb changes nothing until flutter gen-l10n runs. test/app/localisation_test.dart fails when the two drift apart, which is the only reason that mistake is not silent.

And read what gen-l10n printed. It reports some problems as plain prose with no error beside them, and then exits having regenerated nothing at all — so a language can sit in lib/l10n/ looking perfectly fine and not be in the app. If a new language does not appear in the picker, that is the first thing to check.

Dropping a language

Three steps. Only the second is caught by a test, so the third is the one to remember:

  1. Delete lib/l10n/app_<code>.arb.
  2. Remove it from the complete set in test/app/localisation_test.dart, which otherwise fails with app_<code>.arb is held complete and does not exist.
  3. Delete the generated lib/l10n/app_localizations_<code>.dart. gen-l10n does not clean up after itself: the file is left behind, nothing imports it any more, the project still builds and every test still passes — so it would simply ship as dead code. Nothing catches this one.

Then flutter gen-l10n and flutter test. Nothing else needs touching: its entry in kLanguageNames, its section in the glossary and its row in _script are all harmless left in place, and the picker and supportedLocales follow the files that exist.

A player who had chosen it is not stranded. A stored language tag the game can no longer honour falls back to following the phone — the same reading the shop takes of an item id it no longer recognises.

Right-to-left languages

Arabic is one of the thirteen, and its layout is mirrored. Adding it needs no code: Flutter mirrors rows, padding, text alignment and the navigation chevrons from the locale, and the screens were checked at 320 dp in both directions. test/app/rtl_test.dart keeps them that way.

The one thing that must not mirror is the board, and it does not. A carrom board is drawn on a Flame canvas, which has no text direction at all — and the slide bar under it is deliberately direction-blind to match, reading the raw horizontal position of your finger and painting the knob from the left edge. That is what keeps the thumb and the striker on the same side of the screen.

Do not make the slide bar direction-aware. It looks like an oversight and is not. The bar is already mirrored when the board turns round for the second player (barFraction in lib/game/input/bar_mapping.dart); a second mirror from the text direction would cancel it, and the striker would move the opposite way to the finger in Arabic only. test/game/pass_and_play_test.dart drives all four combinations of direction and board rotation for exactly this reason — with the bug in place, every left-to-right test still passes.

What the tests will not let you get wrong

  • A key a translation has but English does not — a typo, or a sentence the app stopped saying — is work that goes nowhere: gen-l10n ignores it without a word. A test refuses it for every language, finished or not.
  • A finished translation missing a key falls back to English for that one line, which reads as a translation that is merely unfinished. The complete set at the top of test/app/localisation_test.dart lists the languages held to the full template; a language joins it when it is translated, and from then on it can never quietly lose a sentence. A language left out of the set must still translate something — an empty stub that puts a language in the picker and shows English behind it fails too.
  • A placeholder dropped in translation — a {name} that does not survive into the translated sentence — is the fault nobody can see by reading a language they do not speak. test/game/game_strings_test.dart runs every sentence in every locale and asserts that every value it was handed comes out the other side. A new language is covered by the fact of its file existing. It asserts presence rather than a count, deliberately, because a language may well mention a number once where English mentions it twice.
  • A language with no name would appear in the picker as a bare tag.
  • A translation that does not fit. test/app/locale_layout_test.dart builds every screen in every language on a 320 dp phone and fails on a layout overflow. This is the one that matters most for a language nobody on your team reads: the usual way a translation breaks a screen is not that it is wrong but that it is longer. It found the prize-table card in nine languages at once.

Plurals, and why the sentences are shaped the way they are

Prizes use ICU plurals, so one coin reads "1 coin" and two hundred reads "200 coins" — and a language with more than two plural forms, as Arabic and Russian both have, gets as many as it needs without any Dart changing:

"prizeWon": "Prize {coins, plural, =1{{coins} coin} other{{coins} coins}}"

Note that the =1 branch writes {coins} rather than a literal 1. It would read the same in English, and in a language that writes its own digits it would not: a bare 1 is a Latin digit sitting beside ১ everywhere else on the screen.

Every sentence the game shows is in the ARB — 208 of them, including the shop's item names, the twelve prize table names, the two rule-set names, the three difficulties and the five countries. The only user-facing text deliberately left out is the product name, the CARROM CIRCUIT wordmark, the thirteen language names in the picker, and the sixty-six robot names, which are people's names. test/app/no_hardcoded_strings_test.dart reads the source and fails on an English literal put back into a widget, naming the file, the line and the sentence.

Screens read the generated class directly through context.l10n (lib/shared/utils/l10n_context.dart). The board is the exception: its message line and result panel are built by pure functions with no widget tree under them, so they are handed their sentences through GameStrings instead.

Every sentence is a whole pattern rather than fragments joined at runtime, because word order belongs to a language. GameStrings (lib/shared/utils/game_strings.dart) is the interface the game asks, and L10nGameStrings is the adapter onto the generated class — it exists because the generated methods take positional arguments and several take two of the same type in a row, so naming them once is what stops a caller handing the second player's name first.

Numbers

Every number the game shows is written the way the chosen language writes it. Seven of the thirteen do not group thousands with a comma — German, Turkish, Greek, Spanish and Brazilian Portuguese use a full stop, Russian and French a space — and Bangla has digits of its own, so 12,500 coins reads 12.500, 12 500 or ১২,৫০০ depending on who is reading.

There are exactly two ways a number reaches the screen, and both go through NumberFormat.decimalPattern so they cannot disagree:

  • Coin amounts, through formatCoins(l10n, amount) in lib/shared/utils/number_format.dart. It takes the localisations because a separator is a fact about a language. Amounts go into the ARB as String placeholders already formatted.
  • Counts — points, a day in the streak, a table number — as int placeholders in the ARB. These need "format": "decimalPattern" beside the type, or gen-l10n interpolates the raw number and you get Latin digits with no grouping in all thirteen languages.
"placeholders": { "points": { "type": "int", "example": "7", "format": "decimalPattern" } }

A numeric placeholder without that line is the easy mistake, because nothing about it looks wrong: the call compiles, the sentence appears, and only a reader of Bangla would notice the digits are the wrong script. test/app/localisation_test.dart therefore checks the rule structurally — every numeric placeholder in app_en.arb carries decimalPattern — and names any that does not. Use an int only where the sentence needs the number itself, which means an ICU plural; otherwise format it and pass a String.

The language picker

First-class, under the profile row in Settings, because a player who cannot read the screen needs to reach it before anything else on it. Languages are listed in their own language — "বাংলা", not "Bengali" — which is why those names are the one piece of user-facing text in the app that is deliberately never translated. How loud a knock is

Section 11 asks for the striker hit to be scaled by speed; the same curve serves every knock, because they are all the same question. The constants are in lib/game/config/audio_config.dart:

ConstantDefaultWhat it does
kQuietestImpact60 Below this a contact makes no sound. A settling board produces a long tail of very soft contacts, and a click for each is a rattle that never ends.
kLoudestImpact900 At and above this a knock plays at full volume. Well under the 2,200 a full-power striker leaves at, because by the time it reaches the pack it has slowed.
kQuietestVolume0.18 How loud the quietest audible knock is. Not zero: a knock fading to nothing has a band where it is playing but inaudible, which reads as dropped sounds.
kMaxKnocksPerFrame3 The opening break lands a dozen contacts in one step, and a dozen clicks at once is static rather than a break. The loudest are kept.

The player can turn sound off in Settings; the switch is read on every sound rather than cached, so it takes effect on the next shot. A missing or unplayable file is swallowed — a buyer who supplies a format the platform dislikes gets a quiet game, not a crash.

Vibration

Light on a knock, firmer when a coin goes down, heavier on a foul, through Flutter's own HapticFeedback — no extra dependency. The player can switch it off in Settings.

Two numbers in lib/game/config/haptics_config.dart stop it becoming a nuisance:

ConstantDefaultWhat it does
kQuietestJolt320 The softest knock worth feeling, far above the threshold for being heard. An ear takes a dozen quiet clicks in an opening break and reads them as a break; a hand reads them as a phone that will not stop.
kMaxJoltsPerFrame1 A vibration motor rises and falls over tens of milliseconds, so two jolts a frame apart are one longer, muddier buzz rather than two.

Settings

Sound, Vibration, turning the board round, the aim guide, the turn timer, and the time allowed for each shot — three lengths (15, 20, 30 seconds) rather than a slider, because the difference between 20 and 21 is not a preference anybody holds. A new length applies to the next game, not the one on screen, and so does a change to the aim guide: both are read once, when the board is built.

One of the specification's settings is deliberately absent: there is no music toggle, because there is no music track, and a switch that controls nothing is worse than no switch.

The aim guide

Section 7.1 of the design asks for a guide that “shows the first collision point”. It is a ghost striker drawn where the striker would first touch a coin, a dot where the two would meet, and a dashed line out of that coin showing which way the coin itself would go. It is on by default — the aid this genre is played with, and a cut angle is the one thing about a carrom board that a phone screen makes genuinely harder to read. A player who would rather read the board themselves switches it off.

The geometry is a pure function, firstContact in lib/game/input/aim_contact.dart, and it is an exact ray cast rather than a simulation: the striker leaves in a straight line, and friction only ever shortens its velocity, never turns it. What it deliberately does not answer is whether the striker gets there — at a feeble power it stops short, and the guide keeps the same silence the aim line does. A test fires the shot it predicts and asserts that the engine's first contact is the one the guide marked, which is what holds the two to the same board.

The coin’s own path is the half that makes a shot aimable. The ring answers “which coin will I hit”, which a player can already see; on a cut, that coin leaves at an angle to the shot, and working out which angle is the part a phone makes genuinely hard. The direction is exact geometry — the line between the two centres at the moment of contact, which is the whole of what an elastic hit between two circles decides — and coinProjectionLength in lib/game/input/aim_line.dart decides how far it is drawn: one fixed kCoinProjectionLength, clipped at the cushion, for the same reason the aim line has one length. How far the coin actually travels depends on a power the guide has no business restating.

Two honest limits, both deliberate. It accounts for no second coin in the way — only the first contact can be answered without running the engine — and it is about two and a half degrees optimistic, because the engine resolves a contact after a sub-step of overlap and so uses a line of centres slightly past the exact tangency. That is roughly 13 units of drift over the 300 that get drawn, against a pocket 30 units across: it points a player at the right pocket and should not be read as finer than that. A test fires the cut it predicted and holds the drawn direction to the one the engine actually sends the coin off in.

The guide sees only as far as the aim line reaches, so a contact past the end of the line is not marked. That is also what makes the striker perk a guide perk, exactly as Section 7.1 words it. The coin’s path takes no reach of its own: the perk already buys a longer sight of the board, and stretching the marked coin’s path as well would pay it out twice.

It is drawn in two inks, a near-white over a dark halo, and the halo is not decoration. The guide is painted over every board rather than chosen per board, so unlike a board's own markings it cannot pick a colour that suits the surface: measured against the lit end of Marble, a near-white mark differs by 0.019 of relative luminance, and against Maple by 0.199 — both under the 0.25 this project demands of board markings. With the halo the worst case across every board skin is 0.451. If you add a board skin, test/game/equipment_skins_test.dart will tell you if the guide disappears into it.

  1. Create a signing key. Keep it outside the project folder, so that deleting a checkout cannot destroy it: 
    keytool -genkeypair -v \
  -keystore "$HOME/carrom-circuit-upload.jks" \
  -storetype JKS -keyalg RSA -keysize 4096 -validity 10000 \
  -alias upload

    keytool asks for the passwords rather than taking them as arguments, so they stay out of your shell history. The 10000-day validity is Google's own requirement: an upload key that expires before 2033 is rejected.

  2. Copy the template and fill in the four values: 
    cp android/key.properties.example android/key.properties

    android/key.properties is git-ignored and must never be committed, shared, or included in anything you distribute — anyone holding it and the keystore can publish an update to your app. android/key.properties.example documents each field.

  3. Build the bundle Google Play expects: 
    flutter build appbundle --release
    The output is build/app/outputs/bundle/release/app-release.aab.
  4. For direct installation or sideloading, build an APK instead: 
    flutter build apk --release

If key.properties is missing, the release build still succeeds but is signed with the debug keys and prints a warning saying so. That is deliberate, so the project builds from a clean clone before you have made a key — but such a build cannot be uploaded to Play, which refuses a debug-signed bundle. Watch for the warning if an upload is rejected:

WARNING  key.properties not found: signing the release build with the DEBUG keys.

flutter test test/app/release_signing_test.dart checks that the fields Gradle reads are the fields the template documents, that no keystore or key.properties has been committed, and that no password has been hardcoded into the build file.

Back the keystore up, and the passwords with it. Without Play App Signing, losing them means you can never publish another update to the same listing — you would have to ship a new app and lose every install and review.

Enrolling in Play App Signing is strongly recommended, and is required for new apps on Google Play. Google then holds the app signing key and the key above is only your upload key, which Google support can reset for you if it is lost or compromised.

FAQ

Do I need an AdMob account to publish this at all?

No. The game is complete and fully playable with no advertising, no account and no network connection. AdMob is how the item is set up to earn, not something the game needs in order to run or to be accepted by a store.

What you must not do is publish with the Google test IDs still in place, because that serves Google’s test inventory and pays nobody. So there are two honest routes:

  • Keep the ads. Create a free AdMob account, put your own IDs in, and set the release flag. That is one short file and two lines of XML, described under AdMob setup.
  • Remove the ads. Advertising reaches the game in exactly two screens, so taking it out is a small job rather than a rewrite. Remove the google_mobile_ads dependency from the pubspec, delete the application ID meta-data from the Android manifest and the iOS property list, and remove the two calls that offer an ad. The rewarded-video panel on the daily reward screen and the interstitial after a prize match are the only two places involved.

What happens to a player’s coins and progress if they reinstall the app?

Everything a player accumulates — coins, the prize-table ladder, the daily-reward streak, purchased equipment, their name and avatar — is stored on the device only. There is no server, no account and no cloud save, and none of it is ever uploaded anywhere. That is also why the privacy policy can say the game collects nothing.

The practical consequence is this. Uninstalling clears the saved data. However, the project does not switch off Android’s Auto Backup, which is on by default, so on a device signed in to a Google account Android may back the saved values up and restore them when the app is installed again. You should treat that as likely but not guaranteed — it depends on the player’s own backup settings and on Google’s scheduling.

If you would rather progress never came back, add android:allowBackup="false" to the application tag in android/app/src/main/AndroidManifest.xml and the data is cleared for good on uninstall. If instead you want progress to follow a player between devices, that needs an account system and a backend, which this item deliberately does not include.

Does the game work on tablets?

Yes. The board is drawn in its own units through a fixed-resolution camera and scaled to whatever screen it finds, so there are no pixel breakpoints anywhere and no tablet-sized layout to maintain. The interface is laid out the same way, and the automated tests build every screen at 320 dp — narrower than any tablet — so the small end is the one under pressure, not the large one.

Two things to know. The game is locked to portrait on both platforms, so on a tablet it stays portrait rather than following the device’s rotation; on a large screen the board is simply a large square with the score panels above and below it. And it has been tested on phones rather than on a physical tablet, so if tablets matter to your launch, run it on one before you publish. Unlocking rotation means changing the orientation in three places — the call in lib/main.dart, the Android manifest and the iOS property list — and the panels above and below the board were never designed for a landscape shape, so expect to spend time on that layout if you do.

Does the game need an internet connection?

Only for advertising. Every other part — the rules, the physics, the robot opponent, the economy, the shop and all thirteen languages — runs entirely offline. With no connection the game plays normally and ad requests simply return nothing, which every screen already handles.

Is there a server, a database or an API to set up?

No. There is no backend of any kind, nothing to host and nothing to pay for monthly. The only external service the app talks to is Google’s ad network.

Does it include in-app purchases?

No. The coins are virtual, cannot be bought with real money, and cannot be cashed out or transferred — they are earned by playing, by the daily reward and by watching rewarded videos. Settings carries a Remove ads panel that says the feature is not available, as a place to start from if you add billing yourself. Adding real purchases means adding a billing package and declaring it on both stores.

Can I change the prices, the rules or the difficulty without writing Dart?

Mostly yes, in the sense that you are editing values rather than writing logic. The twelve prize tables and their entry and prize amounts, the fifteen shop items and their prices, the physics feel, the turn length and the robot’s three difficulty tiers are each a list or a table of constants in one named file. The relevant sections are Economy & prize tables, Equipment shop and Gameplay settings. You will still need to run the project to rebuild it.

Troubleshooting

Gradle fails with "NDK did not have a source.properties file"

The pinned NDK is missing or was downloaded incompletely. Delete the folder it names, then either install that NDK version from Android Studio or change ndkVersion in android/app/build.gradle.kts as described under Requirements.

Build fails after changing the application ID

The Kotlin folder path must match the package declaration in MainActivity.kt. Check both, then run flutter clean followed by flutter pub get.

"Unable to load asset" at runtime

Every asset folder must be declared in the flutter: assets: section of pubspec.yaml. Add the folder, then stop and restart the app — a hot reload will not pick up new asset declarations.

The app crashes immediately on launch

Check that the manifest still contains the com.google.android.gms.ads.APPLICATION_ID meta-data, and Info.plist the GADApplicationIdentifier key. A missing or malformed AdMob application ID kills the process during start-up, before any Flutter code runs. Confirm with:

adb logcat | grep -i "Missing application ID"

Ads never appear

On a debug build with test IDs, ads load only on a device with Google Play services. Check the console for AdMob error codes; code 3 (no fill) is normal for a brand-new live ad unit and usually resolves within a few hours of the unit being created.

The game feels sluggish

Measure on a --release build. Debug builds disable Dart optimisation and are not representative of shipped performance.

If frames are being dropped while the robot is thinking rather than while coins are moving, the cost is the shot search rather than the drawing. A Hard search simulates up to 900 candidate shots, each one played out on the physics engine until every coin stops.

Reach for the engine before the robot. Nearly all of that cost used to be the engine asking questions whose answers could not have changed: on a full board it tested all 190 pairs of bodies for a collision on every sub-step, while only about 2.4 of the twenty bodies were moving. It now leaves alone any pair whose two bodies have both stayed put since it last looked, which is exact rather than approximate and measured at 2.2–3.0× faster on boards with a realistic number of coins on them. If you are profiling a slow search, check first that you have not disturbed that: it is the _stirred bookkeeping in lib/core/physics/physics_world.dart, and switching it off makes the robot think two to three times longer while playing exactly the same shot.

Only after that is the robot's own search width worth trimming. It is aimBudget on RobotProfile.hard in lib/core/ai/difficulty.dart, which is 60 aims expanded into 15 shots each. Lower it and the search gets proportionally cheaper — and the robot proportionally weaker, so update maxCandidates beside it to match (it is deliberately the exact product, so that a search is never cut off part-way through an aim) and reword the difficulty picker, which currently promises that Hard “sees every shot on the board”.

CocoaPods fails when building for iOS

Everything iOS-side is installed by CocoaPods, and the first run is where it goes wrong. Work through these in order:

  • "specs repository is too out-of-date to satisfy dependencies" — your local copy of the CocoaPods spec repository predates the versions this project asks for: 
    cd ios && pod install --repo-update
  • An ffi or architecture error on an Apple Silicon Mac (LoadError, bad CPU type, or a complaint about arm64) — this happens when CocoaPods is running under the old system Ruby rather than a native one. The durable fix is to install it through Homebrew, which gives you an arm64 build: 
    brew install cocoapods
pod --version
    The older workaround of forcing Rosetta — sudo arch -x86_64 gem install ffi then arch -x86_64 pod install — still works, but it leaves you running an emulated toolchain for the life of the project.
  • "CocoaPods did not set the base configuration of your project because your project already has a custom config set" — this one is a warning rather than a failure, and you will probably see it. It concerns Flutter's Profile build configuration, which the stock .xcconfig files do not wire up to Pods. Debug and release builds are unaffected and you can ignore it. If you want it gone, open ios/Runner.xcworkspace in Xcode and set the Runner target's Profile configuration to include Target Support Files/Pods-Runner/Pods-Runner.profile.xcconfig.

If a pod install leaves the project in a confusing state, the reliable reset is flutter clean, then flutter pub get, then cd ios && rm -rf Pods Podfile.lock && pod install.

The AdMob consent form never appears while testing

Almost always correct behaviour rather than a fault. Check these in order, because the first two account for most of it:

  1. You are not in a region that requires one. The User Messaging Platform only shows a form where consent is legally required — broadly the EEA and the UK. Outside it there is nothing to ask, so nothing appears, and the app correctly goes straight to requesting ads.
  2. You have not created the message in AdMob yet. The form is authored in your own AdMob account under Privacy & messaging, not in this code. Until you publish one, UMP has no form to show. See Consent in GDPR regions.
  3. Nothing has asked for an ad. Consent is gathered the first time an ad is requested, not at launch, so opening the app and stopping at the home screen will never trigger it. Finish a match against the robot, or open the daily reward screen.
  4. You have already answered it. Consent is remembered. Either reinstall the app, or call ConsentInformation.instance.reset() once from a debug build to clear it.

To make the form appear on a device that is not in the EEA, use the debug hooks already present at the top of lib/services/consent_service.dart: set kConsentDebugGeography to DebugGeography.debugGeographyEea and put your device's hashed ID into kConsentTestDeviceIds. The hashed ID is printed by the SDK itself the first time you run on that device — look for it in the log:

adb logcat | grep -i UserMessagingPlatform

The line to copy reads Use new ConsentDebugSettings.Builder().addTestDeviceHashedId("…"). Both settings are debug aids — clear them before you build a release, or real users will be shown a form intended for your test device.
